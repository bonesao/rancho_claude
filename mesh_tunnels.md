# Mesh Arch Tunnels — Vertical Growing Structures

Steel mesh bent into walk-through arches, anchored in the ground, used as
vertical trellis for vining crops. **Primary crops: zapallo (pumpkin/squash)
and luffa** — tomato is lower priority here since there are other beds
already handling tomato, so layout should optimize for the first two.

Final arc shape (tall+narrow vs. wide+shallow) not decided yet — building
one arch alone is the bottleneck. Not addressed here; orientation logic
below applies either way.

## Raw material
Mesh currently stored rolled into cylinders, ~2 m tall × ~2 m radius (as
rolled). That describes the **storage roll**, not necessarily the final
arch shape — the roll's coil radius doesn't have to match the tunnel's
walking radius once it's unrolled and re-bent/anchored. Need the actual
sheet dimensions (unrolled length × width) before locking in how tall/wide
each finished tunnel will be — see open questions below.

**Heavy-gauge steel (thick rod/rebar-style mesh, not thin chicken-wire
garden mesh).** Two consequences:
- Bending it into an arch is genuinely hard to do solo — this is a real
  bottleneck, not just an inconvenience. Likely needs either a second
  person, a bending jig/form staked into the ground to bend against, or
  mechanical help (come-along/winch). Worth planning the bending method
  before committing to a final arc shape.
- Once formed, the frame itself will be very rigid — it won't sag or flex
  under vine weight or wind the way lighter mesh would. That shifts the
  structural risk almost entirely onto the **ground anchor points**
  (a rigid arch transfers wind load straight into its anchors rather than
  absorbing it by flexing), reinforcing the anchoring question below.

## The orientation question: does N-S vs E-W matter?

Short answer: **yes, meaningfully** — and mixing both across your handful
of tunnels is worth doing on purpose, not just for variety's sake. The two
orientations produce genuinely different light regimes because you're at
-34.35° latitude in the **southern hemisphere**, where the sun's arc runs
through the **north** sky (opposite of the "south-facing wall" intuition
most gardening content assumes).

### E-W oriented tunnel (long axis runs east-west)
The two curved faces of the arch end up facing **north** and **south**.
- **North-facing side**: gets strong, close-to-perpendicular sun for most of
  the day, all year — this is the sun-drenched side (equivalent to a
  north-facing wall in the southern hemisphere). This is the single best
  face you can offer a sun-hungry crop — more total daily light than either
  face of an N-S tunnel gets.
- **South-facing side**: gets much less direct sun — mostly indirect/diffuse
  light, especially outside of high summer when the sun stays lower and
  further north. Likely **below the ~6 hrs direct sun** that zapallo/luffa
  want for solid fruit set, particularly on the lower half of the arch
  where the tunnel's own curvature adds self-shading.
- Net effect: **one excellent face, one mediocre-to-poor face** for these
  two crops specifically.

### N-S oriented tunnel (long axis runs north-south)
The two curved faces end up facing **east** and **west**.
- **East-facing side**: morning sun only — cooler light, no harsh afternoon
  heat load.
- **West-facing side**: afternoon sun only — hotter in practice (afternoon
  air temperature is higher than morning even at the same sun angle), more
  heat stress.
- Both sides get a genuinely good **half-day of direct sun** each — likely
  enough to clear the fruiting threshold for both zapallo and luffa on
  *both* faces, not just one.
- Net effect: **two good faces** instead of one excellent + one poor.

### Practical takeaway, given zapallo/luffa are the priority
Since neither crop wants a shade side, and there's no strong secondary crop
earmarked for a "poor light" face (tomato is being grown elsewhere), **N-S
is the more useful default orientation here** — it converts both faces of
each tunnel into usable, productive zapallo/luffa space instead of getting
one great face and writing off the other. Cucurbit fruiting also has
diminishing returns at very high light levels, so the marginal gain from
pushing one face from "good" to "excellent" (E-W's north face) is likely
smaller than the loss from dropping the other face below its fruiting
threshold (E-W's south face).

E-W still has a place if: (a) you want to reserve a couple of tunnels for
maximum single-face output — e.g. dedicating the excellent north face to
your best luffa (which needs a full hot season to mature well for sponges)
while treating the south face as a lower-stakes overflow/experiment area
(chayote is a shade-tolerant vertical cucurbit that could actually work
there, or just accept lower yield), or (b) the wind-load logic below ends
up dominating the decision for a given tunnel's placement.

Since these are frost-tender warm-season crops grown Oct–Apr-ish, the sun
is high enough for most of that window that the gap between orientations
is a difference of degree, not full sun vs. deep shade — but it's a real
enough gap to matter for fruit set on the weaker face of an E-W tunnel.

### Crop-specific notes for zapallo & luffa on mesh
- **Luffa is naturally well-suited to vertical growing** — trellising is
  the standard way to get straight, unblemished fruit (ground-grown luffa
  tends to curve/scar). No extra support needed; fruit is light (~0.3-1 kg).
- **Zapallo fruit can be heavy** depending on variety — large types (zapallo
  anco, plomo) can hit 5-15 kg. The mesh itself (heavy-gauge steel) will
  hold that weight fine — the risk is the **plant's own stem/peduncle**
  tearing where the fruit hangs from the vine, not the frame giving way.
  Unsupported heavy fruit swinging in wind can snap its own attachment
  point or strip the vine. Slings (mesh bags, old pantyhose, etc., tied to
  the frame) support the fruit's weight independently of the vine.
  Smaller-fruited types (zapallito, small zapallo criollo) avoid this
  problem entirely — worth choosing varieties with this in mind, or
  planning for slings from the start if going with a big-fruited variety.
- **Airflow through the tunnel matters** — dense cucurbit canopy in a
  humid Buenos Aires summer is a real powdery mildew risk. An N-S tunnel's
  open ends face north/south; if the property's typical summer breeze has
  a north/northeast component (common for the Buenos Aires area outside of
  pampero/sudestada events), that orientation may also flow better through
  the tunnel's length than an E-W tunnel's east/west-facing ends would —
  worth observing actual wind behavior on-site rather than assuming.

### Secondary factor: wind, not just sun
Open pampas terrain means real wind exposure — Buenos Aires-area systems
include the **pampero** (sharp SW gusts, cold fronts) and **sudestada**
(SE, often wet). A tunnel gets denser once luffa/pumpkin foliage fills the
mesh, and a fully-leafed arch catches wind like a sail. A tunnel oriented
**broadside** to the prevailing gust direction takes more lateral load on
its ground anchors than one oriented **edge-on** (long axis parallel to the
wind). Worth factoring into which tunnels go which way, alongside the sun
logic above — not just an afterthought once they're in the ground and
loaded with vines.

### Possible third vertical crop: cowpea (börülce, from the Turkish seed
packet — see [turkish_seeds.md](turkish_seeds.md))
Candidate, not a priority-crop replacement. If it's a **climbing/vining**
type it trellises well (light pods, no sling issue unlike zapallo; matches
the same Oct-Apr full-sun window; non-cucurbit, so it also breaks up the
all-cucurbit canopy behind the mildew/airflow concern above, plus it's a
nitrogen-fixer). If it's a **bush** type it doesn't need mesh at all and
would just take up space better given to zapallo/luffa. Habit isn't confirmed yet — the packet only says "chauchas," which is just
the generic Rioplatense word for a bean pod and doesn't indicate
climbing vs. bush or pod length either way. Most reliable way to resolve
this cheaply: direct-sow 2-3 seeds near a stake/string/the mesh itself and
watch the first 3-4 weeks — twining growth reaching past ~40-50cm means
climber, staying compact/bushy means it isn't. If climbing, best fit is filler for a secondary
"overflow" tunnel face (the same role floated for chayote on an E-W south
face above) rather than competing with zapallo/luffa for a primary N-S slot.

## Open questions
1. **Actual mesh sheet dimensions** (length × width when unrolled) — needed
   to know real finished tunnel height/width/span, since the roll
   dimensions given don't map directly to that.
2. **How many tunnels** ("a handful") and roughly where on the property —
   affects mutual shading/spacing and how much orientation variety is even
   worth planning for.
3. **Anchoring method** — driven stakes, buried footings, concrete? Matters
   a lot given the wind-load point above, especially for whichever tunnels
   end up broadside to prevailing gusts.
4. **Zapallo variety** — big-fruited (needs sling support if grown
   vertically) vs. small-fruited (no extra support needed)? Affects whether
   slings need to be planned into the build.
5. **Spacing between tunnels** if laid out in a row/group — winter sun is
   low enough here (~32° at solar noon on the winter solstice) that
   mutual shading could matter if tunnels are close and used
   year-round, though it's a minor concern for warm-season-only crops.
