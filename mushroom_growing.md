# Mushroom Growing on Logs (Inoculación de Troncos)

> **Project start (2026-05-22):** end of autumn in Loma Verde. This is one of the **best windows of the year** to inoculate — cool temperatures and natural humidity favor mycelial establishment, and the logs will spend their first months colonizing through autumn/winter before being challenged by summer heat. Aim to have all logs inoculated within the next 4–6 weeks.

## Materials inventory

**Logs available (already cut):**
- **Álamo** (poplar) — soft hardwood, fast to colonize, shorter productive life (2–3 years).
- **Fresno** (ash) — medium-density hardwood, the most versatile substrate of the three. Long productive life (4–6 years).
- **Eucalipto** (*E. cinerea*, "eucalipto medallón" / silver dollar gum — confirmed 2026-05-22) — softer and less oily than the *globulus*/*camaldulensis* eucalypts. Aromatic oils are concentrated in the **leaves**, not the wood, so the trunks are noticeably more workable than other eucalypts for log cultivation. Still antifungal — gírgolas is the primary bet — but reishi becomes a reasonable secondary candidate. Productive life will be short (closer to álamo than to fresno) because the wood is low-density.

**Spawn on hand (grain spawn / granos miceliados):**
- Shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*)
- Reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum* / *G. lingzhi*)
- Melena de león (*Hericium erinaceus*)
- Gírgolas blancas (*Pleurotus ostreatus* — the most aggressive colonizer of the four)

### Reasoning behind the table

- **Shiitake** demands a dense hardwood and clean wood chemistry. Fresno is the best match here. Álamo works but the log will be spent in ~2 years. Eucalyptus oils will kill the spawn outright — don't waste it.
- **Reishi** is tolerant and will colonize both fresno and álamo well. Fresno gives longer, denser fruiting cycles. With *E. cinerea* specifically (lower wood-oil content than other eucalypts), reishi is worth a small experimental batch — 2–3 logs at most, treated as a learning exercise rather than a production bet.
- **Melena de león** strongly prefers dense hardwoods (fresno is ideal). Álamo works but expect lower yields and shorter productive life. Eucalyptus is a no.
- **Gírgolas blancas** are the workhorse: aggressive, broad substrate range. They thrive on álamo (one of the textbook substrates) and will also handle fresno. They are the **safest bet for the eucalyptus batch** — *Pleurotus* can metabolize phenolic compounds that block other fungi, and *E. cinerea*'s lower wood-oil content makes this even more favorable than the average eucalyptus pairing.

### Recommended allocation

Roughly, plan your logs like this:

1. **Most eucalyptus logs → gírgolas.** Reserve 2–3 of the better-looking eucalyptus logs for a **reishi experimental batch** — *cinerea* is mild enough that it's worth testing. The rest go to gírgolas.
2. **Best fresno logs → shiitake + melena de león.** These are your "premium" pairings; treat them with the most care during estacionado since the productive life is 4+ years.
3. **Remaining álamo → gírgolas + melena de león (salvaged logs, tapa method).** Melena on álamo is acceptable with modest yields; tapa is the right method (same logic as fresno melena — aggressive colonizer, large surface contact). Barrenado not recommended on álamo for melena.

## Method selection

Two inoculation methods will be used in parallel — each is genuinely better for its assigned pairings, so it's worth running both rather than picking one and compromising on half the batch.

| Pareja | Método | Por qué |
|---|---|---|
| **Gírgolas / cualquier madera** | Tapa con motosierra | Pleurotus es agresivo, aprovecha la gran superficie de contacto del corte transversal, gana la carrera contra contaminantes |
| **Melena de león / fresno** | Tapa con motosierra | Hericium responde igual que Pleurotus — colonización rápida desde una superficie amplia |
| **Shiitake / fresno (+ álamo)** | Barrenado + sellado con cera | Shiitake es lento; necesita múltiples puntos de inoculación distribuidos para fructificar repetidamente durante años |
| **Reishi / fresno + álamo + eucalipto experimental** | Barrenado + sellado con cera | Ganoderma también lento; el patrón disperso maximiza la vida útil del tronco |

### Log dimensions by pairing

Cut logs to size **before inoculation** — once colonization starts you can't easily resize. The two methods want very different log proportions.

| Pareja | Método | Largo | Diámetro mínimo | Diámetro máximo | Diámetro óptimo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gírgolas / álamo | Tapa | 30–40 cm | 10 cm | 25 cm | 12–20 cm |
| Gírgolas / fresno | Tapa | 30–40 cm | 10 cm | 25 cm | 12–20 cm |
| Gírgolas / eucalipto | Tapa | 30–40 cm | 12 cm | 22 cm | 15–18 cm (más masa para superar fenólicos) |
| Melena / fresno | Tapa | 30–40 cm | 10 cm | 20 cm | 12–18 cm |
| Shiitake / fresno | Barrenado | 80–100 cm | 8 cm | 20 cm | 10–15 cm |
| Reishi / fresno o álamo | Barrenado | 80–100 cm | 10 cm | 25 cm | 12–20 cm |
| Reishi / eucalipto (experimental) | Barrenado | 60–80 cm | 10 cm | 18 cm | 12–15 cm |

**Diameter rules of thumb (regardless of pairing):**
- **Under 8 cm** — too thin for either method. Dries out fast, very short productive life, not worth the spawn. Use these as firewood or as foundation logs to keep the stack off the ground.
- **Over 25 cm** — too thick. The center won't colonize before contamination wins. See oversized log handling below.
- **Heart-cracked logs** (large radial cracks already visible at the cut end) — skip for premium pairings. The cracks are direct contamination paths. Use cleanest, most-intact pieces for shiitake and melena; relegate cracked pieces to the gírgolas pile, which can shrug off some bark/wood damage.

**Oversized logs (>25 cm diameter) — split-and-sandwich or cookie stack**

Standard barrenado fails on these: the mycelium can't reach the center before contaminants win. Two chainsaw-based workarounds:

- **Split and sandwich (preferred):** Cut the log into 35–40 cm sections, then split each section lengthwise in half. Spread a generous spawn layer on both flat interior faces, reassemble, bind tightly with a ratchet strap, and seal the exposed seam with wax or packing tape. Supplement with a ring of standard barrenado holes around the outer bark on each section.
- **Cookie stack:** Cut 10–15 cm cross-section slabs, spread spawn between each layer, stack and bind the whole column. More cuts but easier to handle if the grain is too gnarled for a clean lengthwise split.

Interior check is mandatory before committing chainsaw work on old logs — refresh the cut end; clean cream/white interior = viable, dark penetrating stains = discard. Pre-soak 24–48 h if the wood has been ageing for 6+ months.

**Length rationale:** the tapa method colonizes from one face inward, so 30–40 cm is the limit where the spawn front reaches the far end before the wood dries out. Past 50 cm the back portion of the log goes uncolonized. The barrenado method has inoculation points distributed along the length, so a 1 m log colonizes uniformly — and the longer log fruits over many more years.

## Método A — Tapa con motosierra

For aggressive fungi (gírgolas, melena de león) on short log sections.

### Step 1 — Log preparation
- Cut the section to **30–40 cm length**, diameter per the table above.
- **Both ends perpendicular to the log axis** — clean horizontal cuts. The cap must seat flat for a tight seal; a diagonal cut leaks moisture and lets contamination in.
- Cut freshness: inoculate within 1–2 weeks of cutting the section for fresno/álamo. Eucalyptus follows the seasoning protocol below.

### Step 2 — Cut the cap
- With the chainsaw, slice **5–8 cm off one end** of the section. This is the "tapa." Keep the cut as straight and clean as possible.
- **Mark the cap immediately** (paint stripe, chalk arrow, or notch) so you can put it back on the same log in the same orientation — bark patterns line up better that way and the seam is tighter.

### Step 3 — Apply spawn
- Work in shade, clean hands, ideally early morning.
- Stand the log body upright (cut face up). Spread a **0.5–1 cm uniform layer of grain spawn** across the entire cut surface. Don't pile it in the center — even coverage is what makes this technique work.
- For a 15 cm diameter face (≈ 175 cm²), expect ~80–120 g of grain spawn per log. A 20 cm face takes ~150–200 g.

### Step 4 — Cap and seal
- Place the tapa back on, aligning the mark from Step 2.
- **Two nails** (clavos galvanizados, 8–10 cm) hammered through the cap into the body on opposite sides. These hold the cap mechanically.
- **Wrap the seam tightly with packing tape** (cinta de embalar gruesa) — multiple turns around the full circumference, sealing the interface. This tape ring is the real seal; the nails just keep the cap from shifting.
- Optionally also seal the bottom (uncut) end with melted wax — end-grain is a moisture and contamination path.

### Step 5 — Label
Paint mark on the bark with species + date. With four species running in parallel this is non-negotiable.

### Variante — Tapa espejo (logs ~50–65 cm)
For logs too long for a single tapa but too short for barrenado: cut a cap off **both** ends, apply spawn to each face, reseat both caps. Two colonization fronts meet in the middle (~30 cm each), so the log colonizes fully where a single tapa would leave the far end dead. Uses 2× spawn (fine with surplus). Seal both seams; mark each cap to its own end. **Estacionado: lay horizontal** (doble fila acostada) — no plain bottom to stand as a tótem. Optional: 2–3 drilled+waxed holes mid-band to seed the center faster.

## Método B — Barrenado + sellado con cera

For shiitake and reishi specifically — slow, dispersed colonization that yields multiple fruiting cycles per log over years.

### Step 1 — Log preparation
- Cut logs to **80–100 cm length**, diameter per the table above.
- Cut freshness: 2–4 weeks since felling for fresno and álamo (lets the tree's own antifungal defenses subside without contaminants colonizing first). Eucalyptus follows the seasoning protocol below.
- **Bark intact.** Avoid logs with significant bark damage.

### Step 2 — Drilling pattern
- **Drill bit:** 12 mm (1/2") for grain spawn — wider than the standard 8 mm plug-spawn hole, because grain needs more volume to establish than a compressed wooden plug.
- **Depth:** 25–30 mm. Mark the bit with tape.
- **Spacing:** diamond/quincunx pattern, holes **15 cm apart along the length**, with rows **5–6 cm apart around the circumference**. Stagger so no two holes line up axially. A 100 cm × 15 cm diameter log takes 30–40 holes.
- **Drill fresh** — don't pre-drill and let holes sit overnight, that invites contamination.
- **Evitar quemar las paredes del hoyo** — un taladro caliente o una mecha desafilada chamuscan la superficie del hoyo. El carbón en sí es inerte, pero el milímetro de madera deshidratada que queda debajo crea una zona muerta que retrasa el establecimiento del micelio varias semanas. Mantener la mecha fría: **vaciar las virutas constantemente** (sacar la mecha cada 1–2 segundos para que se expulse el aserrín — las virutas atrapadas generan la mayor parte del calor), **baja RPM con presión firme** (rotación lenta corta, rotación rápida quema), y mojar la mecha en agua cada varios hoyos si se calienta. Una mecha de pala / brad-point corta mucho más limpio que una mecha helicoidal genérica. **Rescate de hoyos ya chamuscados:** raspar con un destornillador fino para romper la capa vidriosa, empaquetar el spawn extra firme contra madera viva, y sellar bien con cera — la colonización será más lenta pero no se pierde.

### Step 3 — Inoculation
- Work in shade, ideally early morning, with clean hands. **Wipe the drill bit with alcohol between logs** when switching species.
- Pack grain spawn firmly into each hole with a clean dowel or finger. Holes should be **completely full, slightly proud of the bark surface** — air pockets are contamination entry points.
- Move quickly: inoculate a log within 30 minutes of opening the spawn bag. The longer the spawn is exposed to air, the more opportunistic molds (Trichoderma especially) get a foothold.

### Step 4 — Sealing with wax
- Melt **beeswax** (cera de abeja, ideal) or **paraffin wax** (parafina, cheaper) in a double boiler. Beeswax breathes slightly and resists cracking; paraffin is brittle but cheap. A 50/50 mix is the practical optimum.
- **Si no se consigue parafina:** velas blancas comunes sin perfume sirven perfectamente — suelen ser parafina o estearina (ácido esteárico), ambas válidas. Retirar los pabilos antes de derretir. **Evitar velas perfumadas/aromáticas** — muchas fragancias (eucalipto, canela, tea tree, citronella) son activamente antifúngicas y matarían el spawn. Velas decorativas con mucho colorante también: ignorar.
- **Do not let wax boil** — overheated wax loses sealing properties and can scorch the spawn. Aim for just-fluid, not smoking.
- Apply with a small dauber, stiff brush, or cotton swab. **Seal each hole completely**, with a small overflow onto the surrounding bark — the bark seal matters as much as the hole seal.
- **Also seal cut ends of the log** — end-grain is the main moisture loss path. A wax cap on each end significantly extends log life.

### Step 5 — Label
Same as Method A — paint stripe, tag, or colored screw head.

## Eucalyptus-specific protocol (*cinerea*)

*E. cinerea* is the mildest eucalyptus you could realistically have here — softer wood, oils mostly in the leaves rather than the trunk, and persistent fibrous bark that protects log moisture. Treat it carefully but not paranoically:

- **Season the logs for 4–6 weeks** outdoors before inoculation, partially shaded, off the ground, exposed to rain. This was 2–3 months in the original draft for the harsher eucalypts — *cinerea* needs less.
- Optionally, **soak the cut logs in water for 48 hours immediately before inoculation** to leach surface phenolics. Worth doing if a tank or large barrel is available.
- Expect **moderately slower colonization** — eucalyptus may take 10–14 months before first fruiting versus 8–12 for fresno/álamo. Less of a penalty than with *globulus* or *camaldulensis*.
- **Inoculate somewhat more densely:**
  - **Tapa (gírgolas):** thicker spawn layer — push to the upper end of the range, ~1 cm, and oversize the spawn-to-face ratio by ~20%. More spawn = better odds of overwhelming residual phenolics.
  - **Barrenado (reishi experimental):** increase hole count by ~20% versus the standard pattern (down from the ~30% you'd need on a harsher eucalyptus).
- **Persistent bark advantage:** *cinerea* keeps its bark, so the moisture jacket stays intact through the long incubation. Be extra careful not to damage the bark when handling.

## Estacionado (incubation)

After inoculation, logs spend 6–18 months colonizing before they fruit. This phase is where most home growers lose batches — too dry, too sunny, or in contact with the wrong soil.

### Staging spot (chosen 2026-05-31)
Corner against the house wall, fenced, under eucalyptus canopy, pallets down as off-ground base — good windbreak. **95% mediasombra is plenty** (over-shaded is the safe error). Watch three things, not shade level: (1) keep covers as breathable **shade cloth, not solid lona** — solid plastic blocks winter rain and traps stagnant air → surface mold; (2) brush **eucalyptus leaf litter** off cut faces/seams (euc oils are antifungal); (3) logs on pallets, never on bare soil.

**Mixed shade cloth grade (noted 2026-09-15):** part of the stack has 95% cloth instead of 99% — not a meaningful gap on its own (both are deep-shade grades, well past what's needed). The one real caveat: the eucalyptus canopy overhead is sparse/high (narrow, near-vertical leaves — eucalypts minimize their own self-shading), so don't count on it as a dense, reliable second layer. It's dappled and shifts through the day. What actually threatens colonization is a direct sunbeam landing on wood for an extended stretch, not the average light level. One-time check worth doing on a sunny day: look at the 95%-cloth logs at midday and again early/late in the day for an actual direct-sun patch tracking across the wood (not just brighter dapple). None spotted so far — revisit only if one shows up, then either relocate that section under denser cover or double up the cloth there.

### Location
- **Deep shade**, ideally under an evergreen or dense tree canopy. North-facing wall (south in northern hemisphere terms — i.e. away from the strongest sun) is acceptable. **Direct sun for more than an hour a day will kill colonization** during summer.
- **Wind-sheltered.** The pampas wind is a major drying force. A corner of the property with windbreaks (existing trees, a fence, the henhouse wall) is ideal.
- **Off the ground.** Logs must not touch bare soil — soil fungi and bacteria will outcompete your spawn through the cut ends. Use:
  - A pallet base, or
  - Two sacrificial logs laid parallel as a foundation, or
  - Concrete blocks or bricks under each end.

### Stacking method

You'll have two distinct log sizes (the short ~35 cm tapa sections and the long ~90 cm barrenado logs), so plan **two separate stacks**:

**Long logs (barrenado — shiitake, reishi):** the most forgiving stack is the **"log cabin" / cruceta**:
- Layer 1: two logs parallel, ~50 cm apart, on the base.
- Layer 2: two logs perpendicular, resting on layer 1.
- Repeat up to 4–5 layers high (any taller and the bottom logs deform).

This maximizes airflow and lets rain reach every log. **Don't stack flat** (logs piled like firewood) — inner logs starve for air and humidity.

Alternative for shiitake specifically: **"leaning"** against a horizontal rail in an A-frame. Works well, simpler if you have a fence or low pole already in place. Log cabin is the safe default.

**Short logs (tapa — gírgolas, melena):** these are too short for a proper log-cabin stack. Two options:
- **Tótem vertical:** stand them upright on the base (cap end up, sealed bottom end on the support). They look like little stumps. This is the traditional pairing for the tapa method — gravity keeps the cap seated and the seam tight. Group them in clusters of 4–6 for mutual humidity, with 10–15 cm between logs for airflow.
- **Doble fila acostada:** lay them on their sides in two parallel rows, with the capped end facing inward (away from sun/wind). Simpler if you have lots of short logs.

Tótem is the better choice for the eucalyptus + gírgolas batch specifically — the upright orientation makes any seal failure obvious early (you'll see the spawn through the seam).

### Moisture management
- **Target moisture content of the wood: 35–45%.** You won't measure this directly — proxy is the bark: it should feel cool and slightly damp, never bone dry, never slimy.
- **Buenos Aires summer (Dec–Feb) is the danger window.** Plan to **water the stack 2–3 times per week** during dry stretches — a long soak with a hose at the base of each log, or for serious dry weeks, an overhead sprinkler for 30 minutes.
- Winter and autumn rain are usually sufficient; don't overwater in cool months or you'll rot the wood from the outside in.
- **Cover with shade cloth** (media sombra) during the worst summer heat — 50–70% shade cloth draped over the stack adds a critical buffer.

### Signs of healthy colonization
- **White mycelium visible at the cut ends** within 2–4 months (shiitake, lion's mane), or sooner (gírgolas — sometimes 4–6 weeks).
- A pleasant, faint mushroom smell when you put your nose to the cut end.
- The log feels lighter as the mycelium digests the wood.

### Signs of contamination
- **Green or blue-green patches** under the bark = Trichoderma. Often fatal. Isolate the log immediately, away from the rest of the stack.
- **Black or orange ooze** at cut ends = bacterial or other mold contamination. Same — isolate.
- A small amount of competing fungus on the bark surface is normal and not always a problem; only worry if it's penetrating the wood.

## Sprouts observed on logs — diagnosis (question raised 2026-08-06)

Question: little sprouts spotted on a couple of logs — bad news for the inoculated batch?

Almost certainly **not** the inoculated fungus itself: it's only ~6–10 weeks since the earliest inoculations (late May–June 2026), deep winter, and the expected first flush isn't until autumn 2027 at the earliest (see [Timing summary](#timing-summary)). Two more likely explanations — worth telling apart before worrying:

- **Plant seedlings** (grass/weed sprouts — tiny leaves, no cap/stem) germinating in bark crevices or on the damp pallet base — windblown seed landing on moist wood, unrelated to and harmless to the mycelium underneath. Pull off if they bother you; no action needed otherwise.
- **Wild/competing fungi fruiting** (actual tiny mushrooms — cap + stem, no leaves) on the bark surface — per [Signs of contamination](#signs-of-contamination), surface-only competing fungus is normal and not usually a problem; only worry if it's penetrating into the wood (soft/discolored wood under the bark near the sprout), not just sitting on top. If it's growing directly out of a **tapa seam or a drilled/waxed hole** (an actual inoculation point) rather than plain bark, that's more concerning — it suggests the seal was breached there and worth isolating/inspecting that log.

## Fruiting trigger (cuando empiecen a producir)

Each species has a different trigger, but the **shock principle** is shared: once a log is fully colonized, an environmental jolt (cold water soak, temperature drop, or mechanical thump) signals the mycelium to fruit.

- **Shiitake:** 24-hour cold-water soak (fully submerged, weighed down). Lay on its side in dappled light. First flush in 7–14 days. Re-soak every 8–10 weeks during fruiting season.
- **Gírgolas:** soak shock works the same, but they often fruit naturally after the first heavy rain in autumn or spring without intervention.
- **Reishi:** keep upright, partially buried in damp sand or sawdust to encourage stalk formation. Fruits in warm humid weather (Argentine summer).
- **Melena de león:** lay on its side, very high humidity. Often fruits after temperature drops in autumn.

Detailed fruiting protocols can wait until logs are colonized — revisit this section around **summer 2026 / autumn 2027** depending on species.

### Watering — newly inoculated melena de león (Jun 2026)

Context: ~7 logs inoculated **2026-06-06**, heading into Loma Verde winter. The cool, humid season is working *for* you here — the biggest risk now is **overwatering and rotting the wood from the outside in**, not drying out. Lion's mane mycelium is establishing; it needs the wood damp but breathing.

**Rule of thumb for the coming weeks (June–August):**
- **Default: don't water.** Winter rain + cool temperatures usually keep fresno logs in range on their own. Let the weather do the work.
- **Check the bark, not the calendar.** Press a hand on the bark / cut face: should feel **cool and slightly damp**, never bone-dry, never slimy. That tactile check is the whole watering decision.
- **Only water on a genuine dry stretch** — a run of ~7–10 days with no rain and dry wind. Then give a **short soak at the base of each log** (not a daily misting), let it drain, and stop. Roughly **once a week at most**, and many weeks it'll be zero.
- **Never leave them sitting in standing water** or wrapped in solid plastic — that's how you get sour rot and surface mold. Breathable shade cloth only (already the plan for the staging spot).
- **Keep eucalyptus leaf litter off the cut faces/seams** — the antifungal oils matter most while the spawn is still vulnerable.

**When the regime changes:** the watering cadence in [Moisture management](#moisture-management) (2–3×/week soaks) is a **summer protocol** — it kicks in around December once heat and dry wind return. Revisit then. For now, restraint.

**First milestone to watch for:** white mycelium at the cut ends within ~2–4 months (Aug–Oct 2026). Faint mushroom smell at the cut face is a good sign.
