# Banana Peel Tea (Té de Cáscara de Banana)

## What it is
A liquid fertilizer made by steeping banana peels in water for several days. The peels release potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium into solution. Approximate dry-matter composition of banana peel:
- **Potassium (K): 40–50% of mineral content** — the headline nutrient
- **Phosphorus (P): ~5%**
- **Magnesium, calcium, manganese: useful trace amounts**
- **Nitrogen: low** — like ash, this is not a complete fertilizer

The tea's value is a fast-acting, mild, liquid potassium boost — perfect for the **flowering and fruit-set phase**, where potassium drives flower-to-fruit conversion, fruit size, and sugar accumulation.

Compared to wood ash: same nutrient (potassium), but much gentler, doesn't shift pH, and applied as a drench so it reaches roots immediately.

## Which of your trees benefit most

**Strong fit:**
- **All citrus — naranja, limón, mandarina, quinotos.** Citrus are heavy potassium feeders during fruit development. Banana tea is one of the few amendments where citrus do better than ash, because it doesn't alkalinize. Especially helpful for the **mandarina** (currently bushy but barely fruiting — potassium during flowering can help fruit set).
- **Durazno, ciruela, cerezo, pelon** — stone fruits during the spring flowering window.
- **Manzana, pera** — same window.
- **Palta** — palta loves potassium and tolerates banana tea well, even when small. Apply diluted.

**Less critical for:** trees that are clearly thriving with no inputs (your **naranja maduro**). Don't fix what isn't broken — but a single dose during fruit set won't hurt.

**Skip for now: mango (baby).** Mango is a strong potassium feeder once it's fruiting — in principle a good long-term match for banana tea. But your tree is still a young, tender seedling under frost protection, with no flowering/fruiting to direct the potassium toward. Same logic as the palta entry in `fruit_trees.md`: establishment and shelter come first, inputs come later. Adding a K boost now wouldn't help root/canopy establishment and could even push soft, cold-sensitive new growth right when frost protection matters most. Revisit once the tree is out of the tender/establishing phase and blooming — at that point it slots into the same flowering/fruit-set/sizing/ripening rhythm as citrus above.

  This is really two separate reasons stacked together, worth untangling as the season moves on (checked 2026-08-12):
  - **Frost/tender-growth risk** — fading but not gone. Escobar/pampas frost risk typically runs into mid-to-late September, occasionally light frost into early October, so mid-August is closer to the end of the window than clear of it.
  - **No flowering/fruit-set to direct the potassium toward** — this is the bigger reason, and it doesn't go away when frost does. Banana tea's value is specifically a K boost during flowering→fruit-set→sizing→ripening; a non-flowering seedling has no use for it regardless of weather. So even once frost fully clears, banana tea specifically still isn't the right input for this tree — if vegetative establishment support is wanted at that point, a lower-K, more balanced input fits better. The real trigger to revisit is the tree blooming, not the calendar.

## Best timing

Potassium is useful at **three distinct phases**, and citrus benefit from all three because they carry fruit on the tree for many months:
1. **Flowering / fruit set** — drives flower-to-fruit conversion.
2. **Fruit sizing** — supports cell expansion during active growth.
3. **Ripening** — drives sugar transport into the fruit (higher Brix), improves rind color, and helps the tree harden going into winter. *Often overlooked but real.*

**By tree type:**
- **Citrus (naranja, mandarina, limón, quinotos):** spring flush (Sept–Oct) for flower and set, again during sizing (Nov–Feb), and a final dose during ripening (varies by variety: late-ripening sweet oranges and lemons in your zone often finish April–July). Three light applications spread across the season beat one heavy one.
- **Stone fruits (durazno, ciruela, cerezo, pelon) and pome (manzana, pera):** apply at first flower bud swell in spring, again 3 weeks later. These ripen in summer and finish well before autumn — no late-season dose needed.
- **Palta:** spring through early summer.
- **Mandarina** (currently only 2 fruits): a targeted spring application could meaningfully help fruit set.

**Right now (early May, autumn) — it depends on the tree:**
- ✅ **Citrus carrying ripening fruit (oranges, lemons, late mandarinas):** a moderate K drench is well-timed. Your oranges are in their ripening phase right now — potassium will support sugar accumulation in the maturing fruit and cold-hardiness for the trunk and roots heading into winter. One diluted dose around the drip line is appropriate.
- ⏸️ **Trees with no fruit on them, or stone/pome trees that already finished in summer:** skip — they're slowing into dormancy and won't put K to work. Better to compost the peels or dry/freeze them and brew fresh in spring.
- ⚠️ **Banana tea does not store well** — it ferments, smells terrible, and loses potency within ~10 days. Brew only what you'll use within a few days; dry or freeze the rest of your peels for later batches.

## How to prepare

**Basic recipe:**
1. Take 3–4 banana peels (organic if possible — conventional peels can carry pesticide residue).
2. Cut into 2–3 cm pieces. More surface area = faster extraction.
3. Place in a glass jar or plastic bucket. Cover with 1 liter of unchlorinated water (rain water, well water, or tap water left out 24h to off-gas chlorine).
4. Cover loosely (it needs to breathe but not attract flies — a cloth with a rubber band works).
5. Steep **3–5 days at room temperature**, stirring once a day. Longer than 5 days and it starts to ferment, which produces alcohol/acetic acid — fine for soil but smelly.
6. Strain. The liquid is your concentrate. The spent peels go into the compost pile (they still have value as biomass).

**Do you need to boil?** No. Cold steeping at room temperature is the standard method — the peel cell walls break down on their own and release the minerals. Boiling is purely an optional shortcut when you need a batch in an hour: boil 15–20 minutes, cool, strain. Cold steeping actually preserves heat-sensitive micronutrients slightly better, so unless you're in a rush, skip the heat.

## How to apply

**Always dilute 1:5 with water** before applying. Concentrated tea can burn fine roots.

- **Soil drench:** pour around the drip line, not on the trunk. ~1–2 liters of diluted tea per young tree, ~5 liters for a mature tree.
- **Frequency:** every 2–3 weeks during flowering/fruiting season, max. Not a weekly amendment.
- **Time of day:** early morning or evening, never midday on hot soil.
- **Foliar spray:** generally **not recommended** — the tea can leave a sticky residue that attracts ants and fungal issues. Stick to soil drench.

## Cautions
- **Use it fresh.** Older than a week, even refrigerated, and it goes anaerobic. Anaerobic teas can carry pathogens you don't want in the root zone.
- **Don't seal the jar.** Fermentation produces CO2; a sealed jar can pressurize and leak or pop.
- **Smell test:** good tea smells earthy, slightly sweet, slightly fermented. If it smells like rot, vomit, or sulfur, dump it on the compost pile, don't use on plants.
- **Ant attraction:** the sugars in fresh tea can draw ants temporarily. Water in well after applying.
- **Conventional banana peels** carry residues of fungicides (especially imazalil, thiabendazole) that are sprayed at packing stations. Wash peels under running water before steeping, or prefer organic when possible. The risk is low at low doses but worth knowing.

## Can I use peels that are already decomposing?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no — the smell and look tell you which.

**Fine to use in tea:**
- Peels that are black/brown, soft, and smell **sweet, vinegary, or yeasty** (fermenting). The black color is just oxidation — same enzymatic browning as a bruised banana — and is harmless.
- Peels that have started fermenting have actually pre-released some potassium, so they can make a slightly stronger tea.

**Send to compost, NOT to tea:**
- Peels with **visible fuzzy mold** (black, green, or grey-white fuzz — distinguishable from the flat black of oxidation by texture: mold is fluffy/raised).
- Peels that smell **putrid, sulfurous, ammonia-like, or "rotten-trash."** That signals anaerobic rot, which can harbor pathogens and mycotoxins (especially *Aspergillus*) that survive the steep and end up in the root zone.

**Rule of thumb:** if you'd hesitate to put it near your face, don't put it in the tea. The compost pile is the safe destination — heat and microbial diversity there break down what would worry you in a steep jar. So for an accumulated stash, triage with the smell test before brewing.

**White specks — yeast film vs. mold:** a whitish surface layer isn't automatically bad. Check texture, not just color:
- **Flat, powdery/scaly film** = kahm yeast, a normal byproduct once fermentation kicks in (esp. with alcoholic/acidic smell). Harmless — skim if you like, use the tea.
- **Fuzzy, raised, cottony texture** = actual mold, even if still white (can tint blue/green/black as it ages). Discard to compost, don't use.

**Whole fruit vs. peel-only:** steeping the whole banana/plantain (flesh included), not just peel, adds a lot more sugar to the pot — expect a stronger, faster, more alcoholic ferment than a peel-only batch, and more ant attraction once applied. Not a problem in itself (potassium content is still fine, arguably higher), but dilute more than the standard 1:5 — go 1:7–1:8 — and water in well after applying.

## Plantain (green cooking banana / "patacón") peels — same tea, different behavior

Plantain is the same genus (*Musa*) as banana, and its peel is at least as rich in potassium — using plantain peels instead of or alongside banana peels doesn't weaken the tea. Two practical differences come from it being tougher and unripe (green), not from being a different species:
- **Tougher peel:** thicker and more fibrous. Cut smaller (1–2 cm) and expect a longer steep — 5–7 days rather than 3–5 — for full extraction.
- **Green = high tannin, low sugar:** ferments less vigorously and smells less sweet/boozy than ripe banana peel tea (also less ant attraction). Don't use the same smell/bubbling cues to judge readiness — go by day count instead.

## Practical tip for accumulating peels
If you eat bananas regularly and want to bank the value without brewing every week:
- **Dry the peels** in the sun or a low oven (60°C for 1–2 hours) until brittle, then crush to powder. Banana peel powder keeps for months and can be sprinkled around trees as a slow-release amendment, or re-hydrated into tea on demand.
- Or **freeze peels** in a bag and brew a larger batch when you have a dozen.

For your situation (accumulating raw peels in autumn, with trees mostly dormant): **dry them now, store the powder, and brew tea fresh starting in September.**
