# Wood Ash as Fertilizer

> **Site context (2026-05-02):** soil tested at ≥ pH 8 across the entire homestead using a probe-style tester (the device's max reading). The actual pH may be higher. **Wood ash raises pH further, so on this property ash is the wrong tool for almost every fruit tree.** This document still explains the science and the conditions under which ash would be useful, but the default answer for direct soil application here is "no."

## What it is
Wood ash from a clean wood-burning stove or fire pit is essentially a concentrated mineral residue. Typical composition:
- **Calcium carbonate (CaCO3): 25–45%** — raises soil pH (acts like agricultural lime, but ~50% as strong)
- **Potassium (K): 5–10%** — soluble, fast-acting "potash"
- **Magnesium, phosphorus, micronutrients (boron, zinc, manganese): trace but useful**
- **Nitrogen: ~0%** — combustion burns it off, so ash is NOT a balanced fertilizer

The two headline effects: it sweetens (alkalinizes) soil and delivers a fast potassium hit. On already-alkaline soil like this property, the first effect is unwanted and the second can be obtained more safely from other sources (banana tea, comfrey, compost).

## Which of your trees benefit most

Given the pH ≥ 8 reading, the honest answer is: **none, under current soil conditions.** Adding ash to alkaline soil pushes it further alkaline and locks out iron, manganese, zinc, and phosphorus — the exact micronutrients your trees need to color up leaves and set fruit. Symptoms of this lockout (yellow leaves with green veins, weak new growth, poor fruit set) are easy to cause and hard to reverse.

**Skip — direct ash application is net-negative here:**
- **All citrus (naranja, limón, mandarina, quinotos)** — most pH-sensitive, most likely to show chlorosis quickly.
- **Stone fruits (durazno, ciruela, cerezo, pelon)** — would normally tolerate ash on neutral soil, but here the pH penalty outweighs the potassium benefit.
- **Pome fruits (manzana, pera both)** — same logic.
- **Palta (babies)** — small trees on alkaline soil are particularly vulnerable; palta is already prone to iron chlorosis on high-pH soils.

**Avoid entirely (always):** any future blueberries, arándanos, hortensias (blue-flowering varieties), camellias, or other acid-loving plants — they will struggle on this soil regardless of ash.

**The one scenario where ash returns to the toolkit:** if you ever deliberately acidify a planting bed (with elemental sulfur, peat moss, pine-needle mulch, or sustained organic-matter acidification) and verify pH has dropped below 7, ash can re-enter the conversation for that bed. This is unlikely to be worth the effort for fruit trees at the homestead level — easier to choose plants that tolerate alkaline soil than to fight the soil chemistry.

## What to do with accumulated ash

Since direct application is off the table, you have a few useful outlets:

1. **Give it away or trade it.** Neighbors with acidic soil (less likely in this region but possible in low spots, heavily-mulched beds, or anyone using a lot of pine bark) will value it. Community gardens are another easy outlet.
2. **Save a small amount for slug/snail barriers.** A dry ring of ash around hostas, lettuce seedlings, or other slug-vulnerable plants is a mild physical deterrent (loses effect when wet — refresh after rain). Keep volumes tiny so the soil underneath isn't dosed.
3. **Use sparingly in the compost pile** — at most a thin dusting per layer, far less than the "1 cup per wheelbarrow" rule normally given. The pile's organic acids will partially buffer the alkalinity, but compost destined for already-alkaline soil shouldn't be heavily ash-loaded.
4. **Soap-making**, if you ever go down that path — traditional lye soap uses wood ash leachate. Niche, but a real use.
5. **Dispose responsibly** if none of the above appeals: cool, bagged, into general waste. Do not dump in piles outdoors where it can leach concentrated lye into runoff.

## Storage (if you do keep some)
- **Store dry.** Wet ash leaches potassium fast and turns into useless lye sludge. Keep it in a covered metal bin or sealed plastic bucket.
- **Sieve before storing.** Remove charcoal chunks, nails, unburned wood — they cause problems whether you eventually use the ash or discard it.
- **Label and date** the container; quality degrades after exposure to moisture.

## Cautions (general — apply if you ever do use ash)
- **Never use ash from treated, painted, varnished, or glued wood** (pallets, plywood, MDF, fence posts). Heavy metals and toxins concentrate in the ash.
- **Never use ash from coal, charcoal briquettes, or fires that burned plastic/glossy paper.**
- **Don't combine ash with fresh manure or urea-based fertilizer in the same week** — the alkalinity drives off ammonia (you literally smell the nitrogen evaporating).
- **Wear a dust mask** when sieving and spreading. Fine ash is caustic to lungs and eyes.
- **Don't apply two years in a row to the same patch** without observing the result. Build up gradually.

## Quick decision rule for this homestead
**Default: don't apply wood ash to soil.** The site pH is already too high for ash to help. If you want potassium for fruit set, use **banana tea** (pH-neutral, see `banana_tea.md`). If you want calcium, you don't need to add any — alkaline soil is calcium-rich by nature.

Revisit this advice only if a future, more precise soil test (lab analysis or a wider-range pH kit) shows pH below 7 in some bed — and even then, prefer ash-in-compost to direct application.
